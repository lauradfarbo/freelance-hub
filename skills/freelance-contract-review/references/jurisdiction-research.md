# Jurisdiction Research Guide

Use this once the freelancer's country (and client's country) are known. The aim is a short brief, not a legal treatise: only the rules that change what you advise on this contract.

## Topics to research

Work through these, in rough order of how often they change the advice:

1. **Contractor vs employee classification.** What tests or regulators apply, whether there is active enforcement, and who bears the liability (client, freelancer, or both). This drives the language to avoid.
2. **Late payment.** Statutory interest on business debts, any fixed recovery fee, maximum payment terms (often tighter for large buyers or public bodies), and whether these can be contracted out of.
3. **Termination and notice.** Default notice for service contracts if the contract is silent, restrictions on terminating at the wrong time, and any right to payment for work done.
4. **Penalty and liquidated-damages rules.** Whether a fixed cancellation fee can be reduced by a court, and how to word it so it survives.
5. **Copyright and moral rights.** Default owner of commissioned work, formalities for assigning rights (some countries require a written, signed assignment that names the rights), and whether moral rights can be waived.
6. **Right to suspend work for non-payment.** Whether it exists by default.
7. **Unfair-terms and consumer-style protections for small businesses.** Some countries protect micro-businesses from one-sided standard terms. This can help in a negotiation.
8. **Data protection.** Whether GDPR or a local equivalent applies, and when a processor agreement is needed.
9. **VAT or sales tax.** Registration, rates, reverse charge on cross-border B2B services.
10. **Governing law and cross-border enforcement.** Which law applies by default when the contract is silent, and how realistic it is to sue a client abroad (EU, UK, US states and others differ a lot).
11. **Pending legislation.** Reforms to classification or freelancer protections that may land during the contract term.

## How to search

- Prefer primary and official sources: the statute or legislation portal, the tax authority, the labour or business regulator, or the national freelancer body. Use law-firm explainers as a secondary check, and prefer ones less than two years old.
- Search in the local language as well as English for the key terms. Official guidance is often only in the national language.
- Run separate searches per topic rather than one broad query.
- Rates, thresholds and enforcement stances change, so check the date of every source and say "as of" with the date.
- If two sources disagree, report both and say which is the more authoritative.
- Cite the source for any rate, threshold or article number you state.

## When search is not available

Say so at the top of the jurisdiction snapshot. Give general principles only, mark each local point "to verify", and suggest the freelancer confirm with a local lawyer, the tax authority, or a freelancer association before relying on it. Do not give statute numbers or percentages from memory as if verified.

## Output: the jurisdiction snapshot

Keep it to a brief table or list:

| Topic | What the law gives you by default | What the contract must add | Source and date |
|---|---|---|---|

Include only topics that matter for this contract. End with the one or two jurisdiction-specific risks that deserve a local lawyer's eyes.

## Cross-border engagements

If the client is in another country, add: which law and courts the contract names, whether the freelancer's home-country protections (such as statutory late-payment interest or classification rules) still apply to them, and how the client's local rules might create obligations for the freelancer (withholding tax, local registration, local classification tests). Flag these as escalation items for any contract of significant value.
