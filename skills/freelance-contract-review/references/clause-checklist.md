# Clause Checklist

Contents: 1 Kill fee · 2 Scope · 3 Approval deadline · 4 Late payment · 5 Portfolio and IP · 6 Revision limit · 7 Payment schedule · 8 Rush fee · 9 Notice and termination · 10 Liability and indemnity · 11 Governing law · 12 Data protection · 13 Tax and classification · 14 Other checks

Ranges are starting points drawn from common freelance practice. Adjust to the freelancer's industry, the project size and local norms found in Step 1.

---

## 1. Kill fee (cancellation compensation)

- **Look for:** compensation if the client cancels after work has begun.
- **Benchmark:** 30–50% of the total fee is a common ask. A tiered structure is stronger and still defensible: deposit retained if cancelled before work starts, a larger share of the total fee if cancelled mid-project, and the full fee near completion. Tiered and high is an opening position and often traded down.
- **Local check:** some legal systems treat a fixed sum as a penalty and let courts reduce it. Where that applies, call it compensation for reserved time and work done, and tie it to the project stage.
- **Red flag:** no kill fee at all means the client can cancel at zero cost.

## 2. Scope

- **Look for:** named deliverables, a stated end point, and a sentence that anything else is a change order at an agreed rate.
- **Red flags:** "and other tasks as required", "general support", "as directed by the client". Open scope is a pricing risk and a classification risk, because direction over how you work looks like employment.
- **Fix:** describe outcomes and deliverables, not hours of availability or methods.

## 3. Approval deadline

- **Look for:** written feedback within 5–7 business days, otherwise the deliverable is deemed accepted.
- **Red flags:** no deadline, "acceptance in client's sole discretion", payment tied to approval with no deemed-acceptance rule.

## 4. Late payment

- **Look for:** payment terms (30 days or less is the usual target), interest on overdue sums, and recovery of collection costs.
- **Local check:** many countries set statutory late-payment interest and sometimes a fixed recovery fee for business-to-business debts, and some cap payment terms for large buyers. Find out what applies. If the law already gives you this, the clause only needs to not waive it.
- **Red flags:** 60+ day terms, payment "when the client is paid by its customer", interest waived.

## 5. Portfolio and IP

- **Look for:** the right to show the work in your portfolio, and clarity on whether IP is assigned or licensed.
- **Distinguish:** full assignment, exclusive licence, non-exclusive licence. Each has a different price and different consequences for reusing your own methods and templates.
- **Fix:** if IP is assigned, ask for a premium, assignment only on full payment, a carve-out for pre-existing materials and tools, and an explicit portfolio right.
- **Local check:** default copyright ownership, and whether moral rights can be waived. Some systems do not allow a full waiver, so a broad waiver may be partly unenforceable and still worth narrowing.
- **Red flags:** blanket assignment with no carve-out, a broad confidentiality clause that bans naming the client or showing work.

## 6. Revision limit

- **Look for:** 2–3 included rounds, extra rounds billed at a stated rate.
- **Red flags:** "until the client is satisfied", no definition of what a round is.

## 7. Payment schedule

- **Look for:** a deposit (around 50% on signing for short projects, thirds for longer ones) or monthly invoicing in advance for retainers, plus the right to pause work if an invoice is unpaid.
- **Local check:** whether you have a statutory right to suspend performance for non-payment. If not, write it in.
- **Red flag:** one payment on delivery for anything longer than about two weeks.

## 8. Rush fee

- **Look for:** a definition of "standard timeline" and a surcharge (25–50%) for faster delivery, agreed in writing before work starts.
- **Red flag:** no clause while you intend to accept urgent work.

## 9. Notice and termination

This is often where the real exposure sits.

- **Look for:** mutual notice (not client-only), a notice period long enough to replace the income (2–4 weeks for short engagements, 30–60 days for ongoing retainers is a common ask), payment for all work done up to the end of notice, and deposits or prepaid fees handled explicitly.
- **Termination for cause:** check for a cure period, meaning the client must give written notice of the breach and a chance to fix it before terminating. Check wording carefully: a single qualifier such as "uncured" decides whether you get paid for work after a disputed breach.
- **Ask for:** payment for work in progress and non-cancellable costs on any early termination, and a survival clause so that payment, IP and confidentiality terms continue after the contract ends.
- **Red flags:** termination for convenience by the client with no notice and no payment, client may terminate immediately but you must give 60 days, unpaid notice period, no payment for work delivered but not yet accepted.
- **Local check:** minimum notice rules, restrictions on terminating "indefinite" engagements, and whether long mutual notice periods are a classification red flag in either direction.

## 10. Liability and indemnity

- **Look for:** your total liability capped at the fees paid (or a multiple of them), no liability for indirect or consequential loss, and a mutual cap rather than a one-way one.
- **Red flags:** uncapped indemnity, indemnity for the client's use of your work, indemnity for tax or employment consequences of how the client classifies you (see 13).
- **Local check:** liability for intent or gross negligence usually cannot be excluded, so a clause that tries to is not worth fighting over.

## 11. Governing law and disputes

- **Look for:** your home law and courts, or at minimum a neutral and practical forum.
- **If the client insists on its own law:** treat it as a legal-escalation item for any contract of real value. Ask for arbitration or a neutral venue as a compromise.

## 12. Data protection

- If you handle personal data (marketing databases, CRM exports, customer support systems), check for a data processing agreement, even if you only view data and never export it.
- Missing agreements are a compliance risk for both sides, which makes this an easy ask: it protects the client too.

## 13. Tax and classification

- Check whether prices are stated as exclusive of VAT or sales tax, and whether reverse-charge applies for cross-border business customers.
- Flag any clause that makes you liable for the client's payroll tax, social contributions or penalties if an authority reclassifies the relationship. That risk belongs mainly with the party that controls the engagement terms, and an uncapped version can exceed the whole contract value.
- Independence signals worth having in the text: other clients permitted, your own tools and methods, defined deliverables and end date, you bear real commercial risk, you can send a substitute where practical.
- Warning signs: exclusivity, set hours or location, "reports to", open-ended duration, client-supplied equipment as the only option, "part of the team" language.

## 14. Other checks

- **Non-compete and non-solicit:** keep narrow, short and limited to the client's direct competitors for the engagement, or refuse. Broad ones can block your livelihood.
- **Exclusivity:** usually inconsistent with being independent. Trade it for a significant fee premium or decline.
- **Expenses:** who pays for tools, travel, software, with approval rules.
- **Insurance:** whether the client demands cover you do not have (professional indemnity, public liability).
- **Assignment and subcontracting:** can the client transfer the contract to a third party you have not vetted, and can you use a substitute.
- **Changes to the contract:** only in writing and signed by both sides.
