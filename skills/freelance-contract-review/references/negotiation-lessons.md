# Negotiation Lessons

Practical lessons from real freelance contract negotiations. They are about how to ask, what to concede and what to check, not about any specific client. Use them when writing the negotiation plan and drafting emails or redlines.

## 1. Rank your asks into three tiers

Not every issue deserves a fight. Before sending anything, sort changes into:

- **Needed before signing.** Things that create serious legal, financial or classification exposure: uncapped liability, liability for the client's tax or employment-status consequences, one-way termination with no pay, a clause that makes you look like an employee.
- **Ask for it, accept a trade.** Kill fee level, notice length, payment terms, revision limits, rush fee.
- **Defer to renewal.** Valuable but not urgent items. Keep a written list and raise them at the next renewal, when the client already knows your work and you have more leverage.

Pushing everything at once risks the relationship and the deal. Deferring on purpose is a strategy, not a loss.

## 2. Frame requirements as your standard way of working

Citing a regulation ("the law requires this") tends to send a contract to the client's legal team and slow everything down, because it turns a routine ask into a compliance question. Say instead "this is how I work with all my clients" or "this is my standard freelancer practice". You still get the clause, and it feels like a policy, not an accusation.

Keep the regulatory reasoning for your own notes and for the rare client who asks why.

## 3. Use outcomes, not direction

For classification safety, change wording like "as directed by the client" to "to meet the agreed deliverables" or "as agreed in the statement of work". It costs the client nothing and removes language that looks like employment. Present it as a clarity edit, not a legal one.

## 4. Use your track record as a benchmark

"I have agreed this clause with other clients" is one of the strongest, politest arguments. Keep a private record of terms you have won (portfolio carve-outs, late-payment wording, tiered kill fees). Quote them without naming the client, for example "this is the wording I normally use".

## 5. Portfolio rights are usually negotiable

Clients rarely care if you show the work, as long as confidential information is not exposed. Offer a safe version: no confidential data, client name optional, work shown only after launch or after a delay. It is a standing priority worth asking for in every engagement.

## 6. Open high on the kill fee, then trade

A tiered kill fee (deposit kept if they cancel before work starts, most of the fee mid-project, the full fee near completion) is a strong opening position and is more aggressive than the generic 30–50%. Expect to trade it down. Trade it for something else you want rather than just dropping it, such as a longer notice period or faster payment terms. Present it as compensation for reserved time and work done, not a penalty.

## 7. Check what the client sends back against what you agreed

Near-final contract versions often contain quiet changes. A single word dropped from a clause can reverse its meaning, for example removing a qualifier like "uncured" from a termination-for-breach clause, or reverting a narrowed waiver to the broad original. Before signing, run a proper comparison of the final version against your agreed redlines, not only a skim. Do this even if the client says "only small tweaks".

## 8. Accept that some edits will be rejected, and decide in advance

Standard templates from large companies often resist edits to moral-rights waivers, IP assignments, and indemnities. Decide ahead whether each point is a deal-breaker or a note for the renewal. If a clause is reverted after you narrowed it, log it as an open item for the next cycle instead of reopening a closed negotiation.

## 9. Keep the correspondence short and warm

- Cut clause numbers and legal citations from the email. Keep them in the attached redline.
- Lead with what you are happy with, then list the few changes, then offer a call.
- Drop lower-priority items from the email so it does not read as adversarial.
- Prefer a softer tone over formal legal framing for client-facing messages.
- One clear next step at the end.

## 10. Redline format

A simple, readable convention in Word: insertions in bold red, deletions in grey strikethrough, with a short comment only where the reason is not obvious. Send a clean version as well if the client wants it.

## 11. Data protection is an easy ask

If the work touches personal data in a CRM, email or analytics tool, request a data processing agreement even if you only view and never export data. It protects the client as much as you, so it rarely meets resistance.

## 12. Watch for liability that shifts to you

Clauses that make the freelancer pay if tax authorities reclassify the relationship, or that give the client an uncapped indemnity, can exceed the whole contract value. Move these to the "needed before signing" tier. A mutual liability cap at the fees paid is a reasonable and usually acceptable compromise.

## 13. Know your leverage

A small client who needs you now will usually accept edits. A large company on a procurement template may only change a few things, so choose carefully and use the deferral list. Say honestly which situation you are in.
